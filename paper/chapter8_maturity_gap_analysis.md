## 8. Cross-Cutting Maturity-and-Gap Analysis and Critical Appraisal

The five preceding chapters synthesized the evidence tier by tier. This chapter steps back and reads the tiers together, for two purposes that a tier-by-tier narrative cannot itself accomplish. First, Section 8.1 consolidates the maturity gradient previewed in Table 1 into an explicit map of where the field's effort is concentrated and where it is absent—the central empirical finding the P2G framework was built to expose. Second, Sections 8.2–8.6 identify the issues that *recur across every tier* rather than belonging to any one of them: reliability and hallucination, data and infrastructure scarcity, the absence of standardization and benchmarks, equity and environmental justice, and the AI sustainability paradox introduced in Chapter 7. These cross-cutting issues are, we argue, precisely the class of finding that the conventional life-cycle-stage ontology is structurally unable to surface, because each cuts across every stage of the material flow while belonging to none of them (Section 1.3). Section 8.7 closes with a critical appraisal of the review itself.

### 8.1 The maturity gradient, consolidated

Reading Chapters 3–7 in sequence traces a single, monotonic gradient of evidentiary density that falls as cognitive sophistication and operational autonomy rise. Tier I (Perceive) is established and consolidating, with a dense and still-growing literature spanning closed-set CNN/ViT classification, real-time YOLO detection, and—genuinely large-model—open-vocabulary VLM recognition and promptable segmentation [39–51]. Tier II (Comprehend) is emerging: the enabling methods are mature, but direct, benchmarked USWM applications are scarce, with the adjacent water sector standing as the field's clearest leading indicator [25–29]. Tier III (Generate) is early and exploratory, represented in USWM almost solely by one instrumental application—generative augmentation of perception data [52]—while scenario design, automated reporting, and digital-twin content remain prospective despite mature general-purpose methods. Tier IV (Act) is nascent: open-loop forecasting is well evidenced [53–57,61–65,67], closed-loop optimization and control exist but are narrow and non-agentic [58–60,66], and general-purpose LLM-orchestrated agentic operation has, to our knowledge, no peer-reviewed USWM instantiation. Tier V (Govern) is largely prospective, its content in this review necessarily constructed by importing general AI-governance literature and adjacent-domain exemplars [28,68–75] rather than drawn from evaluated waste-domain deployments.

This gradient is, we submit, the central empirical finding of the review, and its significance lies as much in *why it is visible* as in its content. Under the conventional life-cycle-stage ontology, the abundant perception work of Tier I is distributed across every stage—generation monitoring, collection-point sorting, treatment-line classification, recovery-facility inspection—so that each stage chapter appears comparably well populated, and the reader receives an impression of broadly uniform technological maturity across the waste system. The P2G reclassification removes that distribution and concentrates the same perception studies into a single tier, which simultaneously reveals how much of the field's apparent activity is doing the *same cognitive work* and how starkly the higher-order capabilities are missing once they are no longer diluted across stage chapters. The gradient is not a new fact about the literature; it is a fact that only becomes visible once the literature is organized by the principle this review proposes.

### 8.2 Reliability and hallucination as a continuum-wide property

Every tier above Perceive exhibits a version of the same underlying risk, even though each chapter named it differently: visible misclassification at Tier I gives way to *fluent* failure modes that are harder to detect specifically because the output is well-formed. Tier II's central risk is hallucinated or unsupported regulatory and technical claims, which RAG and citation-to-source exist to mitigate but which remain unvalidated for waste-specific corpora (Section 4.4) [10]. Tier III's constraint-enforcement problem—ensuring generated artifacts respect physical, regulatory, or label-fidelity constraints—is hallucination's synthetic-generation analogue (Section 5.1,5.5). Tier IV compounds both: an agent that acts on a misperceived input or a hallucinated regulatory interpretation converts an epistemic failure into a physical one (Section 6.4). Tier V governance instruments exist specifically because the social and institutional costs of these failures—misinformation, biased allocation, unaccountable automated decisions—do not stay contained within a single tier [68,69]. Treated tier-by-tier, reliability looks like five separate caveats; treated cross-cuttingly, it is a single design requirement—verifiable grounding, bounded autonomy, and human oversight—that must be engineered into every tier above Perceive before deployment, and whose absence at any one tier propagates upward.

### 8.3 Data, compute, and infrastructure scarcity

A second recurring constraint is the scarcity of waste-domain resources needed to adapt general-purpose large models reliably. At Tier I, labeled image scarcity motivated the generative-augmentation literature of Tier III [39,40,52]; at Tier II, the absence of curated, authoritative waste-regulation and operational-record corpora is the immediate bottleneck to RAG-grounded comprehension, exactly where the water sector advanced by building domain-adapted models and benchmarks [26,27]; at Tier III, the lack of waste-specific formulations that encode physical and regulatory constraints blocks generative design and reporting (Section 5.3–5.4); at Tier IV, safe agentic deployment requires verifiable simulation and testing environments that do not yet exist for waste operations (Section 6.3). The pattern across tiers is consistent: the general-purpose model and method are typically already available; what is missing is the curated, domain-specific data, benchmark, or simulation infrastructure required to adapt and validate it for waste. This reframes "more research" as, more specifically, "more domain infrastructure"—a sharper and more actionable diagnosis than the literature's tier-by-tier framing yields, and one taken up directly in the research agenda of Section 9.

### 8.4 Absence of standardization and benchmarks

Closely related to data scarcity is the near-total absence, across Tiers II–V, of standardized benchmarks against which competing systems and claims can be compared. Tier I has converged on shared benchmarks (TACO, WaRP, and the emerging Waste-Bench for VLMs) that enable cumulative, comparable progress [39,40,46]; no analogous benchmark exists for waste-knowledge comprehension, generative-design fidelity, agentic task completion, or governance-instrument effectiveness. The water sector's water-knowledge benchmark [27] again supplies the template USWM lacks. Without such instruments, claims of capability at Tiers II–V are difficult to compare across studies or to validate before deployment, which is itself a barrier to the field's maturation independent of any single tier's underlying model capability.

### 8.5 Equity and environmental justice

The environmental-justice dimension flagged at Tier V (Section 7.3) is, on reflection, not confined to governance; it is latent in every tier's deployment decisions. Perception systems trained predominantly on imagery from well-resourced facilities may underperform in under-resourced ones (Tier I); comprehension systems grounded in formally codified regulation may be less useful where waste management is informal (Tier II); forecasting and optimization systems calibrated on data-rich cities may misallocate collection resources in data-poor ones (Tier IV); and only Tier V explicitly names accountability and equity as design objectives (Section 7.3) [68,69]. Equity is therefore best understood not as a Tier V topic but as a cross-cutting evaluation criterion that the field has, so far, applied almost exclusively at the top of the continuum and almost nowhere below it.

### 8.6 The AI sustainability paradox, revisited

Chapter 7 introduced the AI sustainability paradox—the tension between the environmental benefit large models deliver and the environmental cost of producing that benefit [70–73]—as a Tier V finding. Read cross-cuttingly, however, the paradox is generated at every tier and merely *recognized* at Tier V: every perception model retrained for a new facility, every comprehension system queried at scale, every generative-augmentation run, and every agentic control loop consumes energy and, indirectly, materials. The paradox is therefore not a governance add-on but an accounting problem latent in Tiers I–IV that only Tier V's reflexive stance makes visible. This is, in our reading, the most distinctive substantive contribution that an environmental-engineering audience should take from this review: a life-cycle assessment of AI-enabled USWM interventions—netting model footprint against operational and environmental benefit—is both overdue and, given the field's existing LCA expertise, immediately executable.

### 8.7 Critical appraisal of the review

Honesty about this review's own limitations is itself part of the cross-cutting analysis it argues the field needs. Three limitations are material. First, the evidentiary asymmetry that the P2G map documents is partly a function of true field immaturity and partly a function of search recency and disciplinary visibility—Tier IV and V capabilities may exist in grey literature, industry deployments, or adjacent disciplines not indexed by the databases searched (Section 2.3), and the "near-absent" characterization of upper-tier USWM evidence should be read as "not found in the peer-reviewed and preprint literature surveyed," not as a categorical claim of nonexistence. Second, the qualitative, narrative synthesis adopted in place of a quantified PRISMA flow (Section 2.3) trades precision for transparency about an immature, fast-moving corpus; a future quantitative scientometric study, explicitly flagged as out of scope here, would sharpen the density estimates offered qualitatively in Section 8.1. Third, the tier-assignment protocol (Section 2.2), while reproducible in principle, requires judgment calls for genuinely multi-tier studies, and a different reasonable assignment of a handful of boundary cases (e.g., VLM perception studies that lean heavily on language comprehension) would shift but not overturn the gradient documented here. None of these limitations weakens the central claim that a technology-native organizing framework reveals structure the life-cycle ontology cannot; they bound the precision, not the direction, of the finding. The next chapter converts this appraisal into a structured, tier-organized research agenda.
